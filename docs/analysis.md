# Measures and analysis

What is measured and how `analyze.py` reports it. How to run the simulations is in [running.md](running.md).

## Axes and other measures

Both axes are **efficiencies with the same two reference points**, as ratios of sums over all elections:

`E = Σ(X_method − X_rb) / Σ(X_best − X_rb)`

- **0 = random ballot**: the winner is the favourite of a randomly drawn voter. It needs no assumption about which candidates stand, and every reasonable method should beat it.
- **100 = the best candidate** of each election. Values above 100 cannot be reached; the plots grey that area out. Values below 0 are worse than random ballot.
- The zero is a common level for all elections; it does not judge individual results. Compromise candidates are therefore still rewarded: in an election where the best candidate is nobody's favourite, electing it counts fully toward the numerator.

**VSE** (voter satisfaction efficiency), first axis: X = total utility of the winner, `U(c) = Σ_v u_v(c)`; best = the candidate with the highest total utility. The usual VSE puts the zero at a random candidate instead. With candidates drawn from the same distribution as voters the two differ only in scale, so the order of methods is the same. No utility normalisation: a voter who cares little (all candidates far away) counts little.

**WME** (worst-margin efficiency), second axis, a refined Condorcet measure: X = the winner's worst margin, its smallest head-to-head margin against any other candidate, by sincere preferences, as a share of voters. Best = the candidate with the best worst margin.

- The motivation is legitimacy: after the election voters judge the result, and if someone other than the Condorcet winner won, they can argue that a majority would have preferred another candidate. The worst margin measures how strong that argument is, instead of only whether it exists: losing a Condorcet winner by one vote costs little, electing a candidate whom a large majority prefers a rival to costs a lot.
- Every election counts, cycles included, and the value does not depend on the number of voters.
- With honest ballots Minimax reaches 100 by construction (it elects the candidate with the best worst margin), and all Condorcet methods reach it whenever there is a Condorcet winner, just as they reach 100 on CE. The strategic version WME\* is where methods are tested.

**Reachable frontier.** VSE and WME pull apart: the candidate with the highest total utility is often not the one with the best worst margin, so no rule can reach 100 on both. `analyze.py` computes what any rule could reach in the simulated elections if it knew every voter's sincere utilities: for each weight λ between 0 and 1 it elects, in every election, the candidate with the highest λ × (its VSE contribution) + (1 − λ) × (its WME contribution). Since both axes are ratios of sums with denominators that do not depend on the rule, this traces the exact boundary of the reachable region (lotteries fill in between the points). The plots draw it as a curve and grey out the region beyond it; the report gives its two ends and its most balanced point (highest VSE + WME).

**Reachable region.** The frontier is only the upper-right edge of what can be reached. The whole region is a convex polygon: every rule elects one candidate (or a lottery) per election, so its (VSE, WME) is a point inside the polygon spanned by all combinations. `analyze.py` computes it the same way, for all directions round the circle instead of the upper-right quarter only: for each direction it elects, in every election, the candidate furthest in that direction, and these points are the polygon's corners. The plots grey out everything outside it. This shows that the trade-off is pinned at its ends too: a rule with WME 100 always elects the candidate with the best worst margin, which gives one VSE (up to ties), not anything lower. So a point like VSE 50 / WME 100 is not merely bad but impossible, and the region narrows towards both ends. The report gives the other axis at each end ("with the best WME (100) VSE …"). Three reference points are marked with small crosses where they fall inside a plot: the random ballot (0 / 0 by definition), a random candidate (the zero of classical VSE; its WME is the average worst margin of the candidates) and, since 2026-10-26, the maximal lottery of the sincere preferences (Fishburn 1984; `metrics.maximal_lottery`): the Condorcet winner where there is one, otherwise the lottery over the candidates that no candidate beats in expectation, the optimal mixed strategy of the game whose payoffs are the pairwise margins. It is the probabilistic answer to cycles, with honest preferences known; run.py stores its expected utility and worst margin per election (columns U_ml, WM_ml; runs from before have no cross). The report gives the values of the random candidate and the maximal lottery.

A method's distance to the curve is what it loses to having only ballots, not utilities, and to strategy. Faint dashed lines mark 1, 2, 4 and 8 points below the frontier (labelled −1 … −8 at their lower end): each is the frontier shifted down and left by that many points on both axes, so a method on the −4 line could gain 4 points of VSE *and* 4 of WME before it reached the frontier. A strategic shift parallel to these lines is a trade between the two measures; one that crosses them inward is a loss on both. They are drawn inside the region only: beyond the frontier's ends the region bends away. Without a frontier (chain and track plots over conditions with different electorates) the lines follow the 100 / 100 corner instead. For this, run.py stores every candidate's total utility and worst margin per election (columns U_cands, WM_cands, and Un_cands for the normalized line below).

**Normalized ballots** (a dotted line, labelled "normalized ballots"): what the same rule reaches when it sees each voter's utilities only as the voter would put them on a ballot, stretched between their least and most liked candidate (0 to 1). That keeps every pairwise preference, so the WME end is the frontier's, but loses how much one voter cares compared with another: at its VSE end the rule elects the candidate with the highest total of normalized utilities, which honest Score on unrounded ballots would elect. From there the line drops straight down: with normalized ballots, giving up more WME buys no more VSE, so on that side it is a wall (drawn inside the reachable region only). The report gives that end ("with normalized ballots at most VSE …"). The VSE region to the right of the line needs information no ballot carries when voters use their own scale; rounding to the score range comes on top. It is not a strict bound: it takes the normalized ballots at face value, and with few elections a method can land a point or so beyond it. Runs from before 2026-10-20 lack the column and show no line.

**Rounded ballots** (a dash-dotted line, since 2026-10-27; column Ur_cands): the same line with the normalized ballots rounded to the score range of the run (`--max-score`), that is with the honest score ballots instead of the unrounded ones (equal totals split evenly, as Score splits them). The gap between the two lines is what the coarse scale costs on its own. In a max-score sweep the line differs per condition, so the chain plots leave it out. With few elections the two lines can cross by a few tenths, within the noise.

**Further reference points** (since 2026-10-27; columns U_\<key\>, WM_\<key\>): rules that see the sincere preferences or the positions, as benchmarks, not proposals, drawn as crosses with the others:

- **CW//random ballot** (`cwrb`): the Condorcet winner if there is one, else a random voter's favourite. What electing the Condorcet winner is worth on its own.
- **CW//utility winner** (`cwuw`): the Condorcet winner if there is one, else the candidate with the highest total utility. The best a rule can do while always respecting the Condorcet winner; with Condorcet winners in nearly every election it lies close to the previous one.
- **random pair** (`pair`): two candidates drawn at random, the sincere majority decides. The simplest majority-based lottery (not the method Random pair runoff, which draws two ballots).
- **median voter** (`median`): the candidate nearest the voters' geometric median, the median voter in several dimensions; **mean voter** (`mean`): the candidate nearest the voters' mean, the utility winner under quadratic utility. Where "the centre" lands on the two axes. For the ks models positions are scaled by the voters' average weights first (as in show_electorates.py).

**What the plots draw** (`--overlay`): `frontier` (the reachable region and the 100 lines), `guides` (−1 … −8), `normalized`, `rounded`, `points` (reference points; `--points` chooses which: rb, rc, ml, cwrb, cwuw, pair, median, mean; by default all but ml and cwuw, which lie next to cwrb in the runs so far). Default: all; `--overlay` with no names draws only the methods.

**Condorcet efficiency (CE)**, reported alongside but not an axis: among elections with a Condorcet winner by sincere utilities, the probability the method elects it. It ignores elections without a Condorcet winner and does not distinguish narrow from clear ones.

**Low** (and Low\* with learned ballots), reported alongside but not an axis: the share of elections whose winner lies below the median candidate by total utility (for a lottery, its expected total utility below the median of the candidates' totals). VSE and WME are averages over elections, ratios of sums, so a method that is usually excellent and sometimes disastrous can match one that is always good; Low shows how often a method elects a poor candidate. After John Huang's votesim, which reports the worst 5% of elections; a share of poor outcomes is used instead because one election's VSE is unstable (its denominator can be small). Computed from the candidates' totals stored in every row (U_cands), so older runs with that column get it too.

VSE, WME and CE use sincere ballots; VSE\*, WME\* and CE\* (in the CSV: VSE_learned, WME_learned, CE_learned) the strategic ballots ("learned": after the poll rounds, or after convergent learning). In all cases the preferences that count are the sincere ones.

**Strategy types** (`strategy.strategy_types`), among voters whose learned ballot reverses at least one real preference:
- *compromise*: the favourite is not at the top, and the best-liked top candidate is liked above the voter's mean (a viable second choice raised);
- *push-over*: the favourite is not at the top, and every top candidate is liked below the mean (a weak candidate raised, to steer eliminations or runoffs);
- *burial*: the favourite stays at the top, but a preference further down is reversed.

The three are exclusive. For score ballots, "burial" includes small reversals among lower-rated candidates, so a high burial share can go with high sincerity.

**Sincerity and fidelity.** Two separate questions about a learned ballot: does it go against the voter's own preferences, and how much does it tell about them?

**Sincerity** (`strategy.sincerity`) is about voting against one's own preferences because it pays. Per voter, the *reversal mass* R of a ballot is the sum of `u[a] − u[b]` over the pairs the voter prefers a > b but the ballot puts b above a. Sincerity is `1 − R(ballot) / R(worst)`, where the worst ballot is the most dishonest one the method's ballot format allows for this voter: a vote for the last choice in plurality, a veto against the favourite, the reversed ranking (rankings, and scores with enough levels), the most damaging cut in approval (found exactly, over all cuts), the reversed order in three levels for three grades, the three least liked ranked in reverse for IRV-3. So 1 means no reversed preference and 0 means as far against one's own preferences as this format permits, the same in every format. Ties are not reversals and cost nothing: exaggeration (5, 3, 1, 0 → 5, 5, 0, 0), an approval threshold anywhere, unranked candidates. They say less, not something false, and in approval or plurality there is no single honest place for the threshold anyway. This is the usual definition of a sincere ballot (Brams and Fishburn: no candidate is put above one the voter likes more), scaled per format. Wolitzky ([Fully sincere voting](https://economics.mit.edu/sites/default/files/publications/fullysincerevoting.pdf), *Games and Economic Behavior* 67(2), 2009, 720–735) gives the corresponding property of a voting rule: it is *pairwise sincere* if a voter prefers to cast a vote for y over z exactly when they prefer y to z, so that no reversal ever pays; with approval ballots, voters then choose a high set (every approved alternative preferred to every unapproved one). The lab measures how far the learned ballots depart from this, per format, rather than asking whether a rule guarantees it.

**Fidelity** (`strategy.fidelity`) is about how much of the voter's utility differences the ballot conveys. Utilities are stretched to 0–1 per voter (favourite 1, least liked 0) and compared with the values the ballot implies: scores and grades by their value, rankings evenly spaced, approval and plurality marks as 1 and 0, veto 0 for the vetoed candidate. Over all pairs, fidelity is `1 − Σ |Δu − Δv| / Σ 2 |Δu|` (Δu, Δv the differences in utility and implied value): 1 if the ballot shows the differences exactly; a tie loses half of a pair; a mirrored pair loses all of it. A sincere ranking of utilities 100 / 3 / 2 / 1 / 0 loses a lot, since it implies 100 / 75 / 50 / 25 / 0. Fidelity therefore includes what the format cannot express: the report gives it for the learned ballots and for the sincere ones ("(honest)"), so the format's own loss and what strategy adds can be told apart. It is reported for context, not used for the front.

Example, utilities 0.5 / 0.3 / 0.15 / 0.05:

| Ballot | Sincerity | Fidelity |
|---|---|---|
| Plurality: vote for the 1st choice (sincere) | 1 | 0.72 |
| Plurality: vote for the 2nd choice | 0.75 | 0.32 |
| Plurality: vote for the last choice | 0 | 0.05 |
| Ranking, sincere | 1 | 0.93 |
| Ranking: 2nd moved above the 1st | 0.87 | 0.63 |
| Ranking: reversed | 0 | 0 |
| Score 0–5: sincere 5, 3, 1, 0 | 1 | 0.97 |
| Score 0–5: exaggerated 5, 5, 0, 0 | 1 | 0.70 |
| Approval: {1st, 2nd} (sincere) | 1 | 0.70 |
| Approval: {3rd, 4th} | 0 | −0.10 |

The exaggerated score ballot and the approval ballot say the same and get the same values. Two-round ballots (TTR, the approval runoffs): the voter only chooses the round-1 marks (the runoff ranking is the sincere one), so both measures look at the marks, as a plurality or approval ballot. **Undefined for TRASH and the Inverse-\* methods.** Both measures assume that the honest ballot is the one a voter would want to keep, so moving away from it is a cost of strategy. In TRASH (the candidate ranked lower wins) and the Inverse-\* methods (honest voting elects the worst candidate) the honest ballot works against the voter's own interest: what a ballot says and whom it helps point opposite ways, and leaving the honest ballot is escaping a bad method, not giving up honesty. Sincerity and fidelity are therefore left blank for these methods (`methods.sincerity_defined`), in the CSV, the tables (—) and the plots; on the front they count as ties on the sincerity axis. The strategy types (compromise, burial, push-over) are still reported; for the Inverse-\* methods they are measured against the reversed preferences the honest ballot expresses. CSVs from before 2026-10-12 (no fidelity column) hold an earlier sincerity measure (reversals over all pairs, not scaled per format); the report says so.

**Condorcet winner in the cast ballots** (`metrics.ballot_condorcet`). Studies of real ranked-ballot elections compute the Condorcet winner from the cast ballots, which may themselves be strategic. The model knows both: the real Condorcet winner (sincere preferences) and the one the ballots show (a voter counts for a over b if the ballot puts a higher; equal = no preference). Per election and method (columns `bcw`, `p_bcw`): the ballots show the same Condorcet winner, another one (false), none although there is a real one (a cycle or ties), or one although there is none (spurious); and how often the method elects the ballots' Condorcet winner, which is the Condorcet efficiency ballot data would report. Only for ballots that record an order (rankings, scores, grades); plurality, approval, veto and two-round marks are left blank, since their pairwise counts mostly reflect the marks. For the Inverse-\* methods the ballots are read reversed. The report has a table per condition, with the honest ballots in brackets: for rankings these always show the real winner, for scores and grades rounding and ties can already hide or change it.

**Ties** (`metrics.ties`, since 2026-10-26). Two per election and method (columns `split`, `zero_margins`). **Split**: the winner is drawn by lot among several candidates, because the method breaks a tie at random or draws by design (Condorcet-random-drop; Ranked pairs and Kemeny-Young with equal margins or several optimal rankings). After strategic nomination, a tie between a clone and its original is no split, as both count as the original. **Tied pairs**: the share of candidate pairs that the cast ballots leave tied (as many voters put a above b as b above a), counted as for the Condorcet winner in the ballots: on the field the voters face, and only for ballots that record an order (blank for plurality, approval, veto and two-round marks). Tied pairs come from ballots that allow equal ratings (coarse score scales, grades, truncated rankings, learned ties in the ER methods) and from even vote counts. They matter where a rule has to settle pairwise ties (BTR-score, Smith//Score, the Condorcet methods); a split counts how often the rule ends in a lottery. The report has a table per condition with the share of elections with a split, with at least one tied pair, and the share of tied pairs, for learned ballots with the honest ones in brackets; summary.csv has the same (`split`, `zero_any`, `zero_pairs`, each also `_honest`). Runs from before have no table and blank columns.

## Analysis

`analyze.py` reads one or more elections CSVs. Rows with the same parameters except the seed form one **condition**; seeds are pooled as replications.

**Uncertainty: paired bootstrap.** All methods face the same electorates, so each bootstrap replicate redraws whole elections with replacement and recomputes every method on that same draw (1000 replicates by default). The draws depend only on the condition's elections and `--seed`, not on what else is analysed: a condition gets the same draws in every report and in `compare.py`, so adding a run to an analysis does not change the other conditions' results (until 2026-10-25 one random stream ran through all conditions, and a borderline dominance could change with the set of inputs). This keeps comparisons paired: two methods can differ clearly even when the interval of each value on its own is wide, because elections differ much more from each other than methods differ within an election.

**The front: VSE\*, WME\* and sincerity.** What counts is the result with strategic voters and how honest they can afford to be. Honest VSE and WME depend more on modelling assumptions (who votes how sincerely is itself a model) and are reported for context, not for dominance. Above each condition's table the report gives two sets. The **Pareto front**: methods no other method matches or beats on all three point estimates (at least as good on all, better on one), however small the difference, except that sincerity differences below 1 point count as ties. **Undominated**: methods no other method clearly beats (see Dominated by, below), so noise-sized differences count as ties. The two need not agree: a tiny advantage keeps a method on the Pareto front but does not protect it from a method clearly better on another axis. summary.csv marks the Pareto front (`pareto_front`). The table is sorted by VSE\*.

**What is reported per condition and method:**

| Output | Meaning |
|---|---|
| VSE, WME, VSE\*, WME\*, CE, CE\*, Low, Low\*, sincerity | point estimate and 95% interval of each value on its own |
| dominated by | methods clearly better on at least one of the three front axes (VSE\*, WME\*, sincerity) and clearly worse on none, each with the axes on which it is better; the report shows at most three, the CSV all. Clearly: the paired 95% interval of the difference excludes 0, smaller differences count as ties. Without this, methods level on one axis would be kept apart by noise-sized differences. Sincerity is measured very precisely, but below 1 point (99.7 against 99.9, say) no voter is noticeably more honest, so such differences count as ties as well (`metrics.FRONT_TOLERANCE`; before 2026-10-21 any clear sincerity difference counted) |
| adaptive voters (with `--adaptive`) | share of the adaptive voters who turned strategic, per method |
| manipulation (with `--manipulation`) | share of elections in which a coalition can manipulate from honest ballots, one- and two-sided, and the VSE of the worst successful manipulations ([model.md](model.md), Manipulation test) |
| paired differences (`--compare "A:B"`) | difference A − B per axis with a 95% interval; marked as a tie when the interval includes 0 |

**How to interpret:**
- The values are the result. Whether a method lies on the Pareto front is already interpretation: it depends on which axes count and on which methods are included. "Dominated by" is only a pointer to where to look.
- The axes are kept separate on purpose. Each carries its own value judgement (utilitarian or majoritarian, honest or strategic); no weighting into a single score.
- Read results **per condition first**. The strategic axes depend on how voters strategise: the number of poll rounds, the aggressive and strategic shares (and, with `--converge`, the group size; see [model.md](model.md), Coordination).
- An ordering between two methods only counts if their paired difference is not a tie.

Sweep plots appear automatically for every parameter that has at least two conditions differing from the base only in that parameter: a one-at-a-time sweep (`sweep.py --one-at-a-time`) or a grid over a single parameter. Parameters that are only ever changed together, such as `--exits 1 --clones 2` against the base, form one chain (`chain-exits-clones.png`, values shown as 0 / 0 → 1 / 2); in a grid, where each also varies on its own, the diagonal is not drawn as a chain. They show point estimates only; with few elections the zigzags in a chain are mostly noise. One far-off method (often Plurality) sets the shared scale for all panels; `--hide` or `--show` zooms in.

Methods are identified by labels, not colour. Random pair runoff, Anti-plurality and TRASH are left out of the scatter plots by default (`--hide`), since their distance compresses the scale; they stay in tables and heatmaps. `--hide` with no names shows all.

Sample size: with 160 elections per condition the intervals of single values are about ±3 points and many paired differences are still ties. For conclusions, several hundred elections per condition and at least two seeds are advisable.

## Robustness: one parameter at a time

To see how much a model assumption matters, vary each parameter on its own around a base condition and check whether the methods keep their order:

```
python sweep.py --one-at-a-time --noise-strength 5 3 7 --dims 6 10 --width 0.7 1.0 \
    --rounds 1 3 --aggressive 0.5 0 1 --strategic 0 0.5 1 --seeds 1 2 --elections 400 --out model
python analyze.py model                                   # -> output/model/analysis/
```

Conditions are labelled by how they differ from the base (`c1: base`, `noise strength 3.0`, ...).

`analyze.py` adds a **rank stability** table when there are several conditions: for each axis, the rank correlation (Spearman) between the methods' values in a condition and in the base (the first condition). The methods hidden in the plots are left out. Its first row is a noise reference: the agreement expected from sampling noise alone between two runs of the base condition (the base's even and odd elections correlated, then scaled up to full size with the Spearman-Brown formula `2r / (1 + r)`; shown as — when the halves' correlation is 0 or below, where the formula has no meaning: the base gives no stable order on that axis, e.g. a few methods with nearly equal values). A condition near or above that row orders methods as the base does; a value clearly below it means the parameter changes which methods do well. With few elections everything is noise, and VSE\* in particular needs many elections (several hundred per condition): strategic differences between methods are small. Many methods lie close together on each axis, so rank correlation is a strict test; the heatmap shows where the changes come from.

An **electorates** table (and a line in each condition's section) describes the generated electorates themselves, independent of the methods: how often a Condorcet winner exists, how often it also has the highest total utility, how much VSE electing it loses (ratio of sums, as VSE), and how steep preferences are (u(favourite) ÷ u(least liked), median over voters, then median over elections). The third column explains most VSE differences between Condorcet and rated methods; the last one is a concrete handle for choosing `--width`.
