# The model

How electorates, ballots and strategic voters are modelled. Running the simulator is in [running.md](running.md), the measures and the analysis in [analysis.md](analysis.md), the methods in [methods.md](methods.md).

## Electorates

Each election draws a fresh electorate from the model chosen with `--model` (`models.py`, one entry per model in `MODELS`). A model draws only the positions of voters and candidates (and, if it has them, how much each person weighs each dimension); distances, utilities and the two candidate parameters below are the same for every model. The model and its own parameters are written into every row and named in the report; runs from before models existed are read as `clusters`. Common to all models: the distance is Euclidean (each dimension scaled by the person's weight for it, where the model has weights), and the utility is `u = exp(-distance / width)`, absolute and not normalised: 1 at zero distance, towards 0 far away. A smaller `--width` makes voters care only about nearby candidates. With this fall-off a voter far from every candidate has small utility differences between them and counts little in VSE, while their ballot still spans the whole range. `--utility linear` uses `u = -distance / width` instead (as vse-sim): every unit of distance counts the same, near or far, and `--width` only sets the scale (no result changes with it). It is the limit of the exp fall-off for a very wide width. `--utility quadratic` uses `u = -(distance / width)^2`: each further unit of distance costs more (risk aversion), so far voters count most. The three shapes put the best candidate by total utility at different centres of the electorate: exp near a dense group of voters (the mode), linear near the spatial median, where the Condorcet winner sits in one dimension, quadratic near the voters' mean. Preferences (every voter's order of the candidates) are the same under all three, so the Condorcet winner and WME's reference are too; only utility differences, and with them VSE, honest score ballots and the stakes of strategy, change. The favourite ÷ least liked ratio is not defined for linear and quadratic utility (shown as —).

Five models are available: `noise` (the default), `clusters`, `field`, `ks` and `ks-quinn`. Each is described below, the default first.

**Nested electorates** (since 2026-11-03). Every model draws what the electorate is made of (the noise, cluster centres, issue bundles, fields) from one random stream of the election, and each voter's and each candidate's draws from streams of their own, in order. So the first 99 voters of an electorate of 199 are the electorate of 99. By default the candidates are voters too (Candidates who vote, below): the first voters, so the field is the same whatever the number of voters, the first 6 candidates of a field of 10 are the field of 6, and the voters do not depend on the number of candidates. With candidates apart from the voters (`--cands-vote 0`) the same holds, except that in the ks models the voters change with the number of candidates, since their factions are drawn after the candidates'. Tests that change the number of voters or of candidates therefore compare the same elections, with voters or candidates added, and their differences are paired as for every other test. Until then every number of voters or candidates gave unrelated electorates, and a sweep over the number of voters showed mainly the differences between those samples. In the noise model the voters and candidates are drawn from the same cloud of 2000 points for up to 1000 persons; in the field model they live at places of one Lévy flight of 1000 steps.

**Defaults, and why** (2026-10-16; [journal.md](journal.md), Electorate models). The defaults were chosen so that the electorates are plausible against two kinds of real data and still separate the methods:

- `noise`, because it has the fewest assumptions of the models and gives a balanced electorate: `clusters` is clumpy (with few clusters, islands), `ks` is uniform and low-dimensional (two or three dimensions drawn, in which VSE and WME hardly pull apart), and `field` gives a featureless blob.
- Strength 5, 6 dimensions, width 0.7. A Condorcet winner exists in about 97% of elections, as in real ranked elections with 50 to 100 or more voters (96–98% in Myers' CIVS data). The candidates' map from approval ballots (`--sav`, below) is close to Alós-Ferrer & Granić's real one with 6 candidates, if somewhat more concentrated on the first dimension. Electing the Condorcet winner loses about 15% of VSE, so VSE and WME pull apart and the methods differ. From strength 5 to 7 the method order stays within its sampling noise, so the exact value matters little. Below 5 (with 10 dimensions and width 1.0, below 6) the electorates become easy: nearly every method is close to the best possible result.
- The models' own defaults are those of their best variant in the comparisons: `clusters` with 20 clusters, no Zipf, fixed total variance and salience per cluster with exponent 0.5; `field` and `ks` unchanged.
- Before 2026-10-16 the defaults were `clusters`, 10 dimensions with Zipf, 5 clusters, no salience, unscaled positions and width 1.0; to reproduce older results, give these explicitly (each results file records every parameter).

**Fractal noise** (`--model noise`, the default). Voters are dense where a fractal noise is high, like Perlin noise with several octaves, so they gather in clumps within clumps at every scale. There is no number of clusters, no decay law for the dimensions and no salience to choose:

- The noise, new for every election: five octaves of eight plane waves each, in random directions in the `--dims`-dimensional issue space. From one octave to the next the frequency doubles and the amplitude falls by 2^−0.5. (This is fractal noise built from random waves; Perlin's own lattice construction is impractical in more than three dimensions.)
- People are drawn from a standard Gaussian weighted by exp(`--noise-strength` × noise). At 0 that is a plain Gaussian blob; larger values give sharper clumps, and electing the Condorcet winner becomes costlier while a Condorcet winner stays about as frequent.
- Sampling: 2000 points start from the Gaussian; the strength is raised in ten steps, and each time the points are resampled by their gain in weight and then take five small random steps (Metropolis), so that clumps get their real width. Voters (and, with `--cands-vote 0`, the candidates) are distinct points drawn from the result.
- Positions are scaled so the dimensions' total variance is π²/6, as `--cluster-fixed-variance` does for clusters. Drawing one electorate takes about 0.1 s, longer than the other models, but little next to the methods' strategy.
- The octaves, waves, amplitude decay and sampling settings are fixed in `models.py` (`NOISE_*`). `--noise-strength` is the knob that matters.

**Voter clusters** (`--model clusters`):

- cluster centres: `--cluster-count` points, normal in `--dims` dimensions;
- cluster sizes: random weights from a flat Dirichlet distribution, so some elections have one dominant cluster and others are balanced;
- voters: each assigned to a cluster by these weights, placed normally around its centre with standard deviation `--cluster-spread` (per dimension, before the Zipf scaling);
- candidates (with `--cands-vote 0`; by default they are voters): drawn from the same distribution as voters (cluster by the same weights, same spread), unless moved by the two candidate parameters below.

**Zipf dimensions** (`--cluster-zipf 1`; the default until 2026-10-16), after Chris Smith's Mixture of Zipf Gaussians ([article](https://cdsmithus.medium.com/simulating-elections-with-spatial-voter-models-1ff50892390), [code](https://github.com/cdsmith/spatial-voting), `src/Model.hs`): dimension i (1, 2, 3, ...) has standard deviation 1/i — for cluster centres, voters and candidates alike — so one issue dominates and the others matter less and less, without being absent.

- Scaling follows Smith's code: the standard deviation is 1/i, so the variance is 1/i². (The article's wording "proportional to 1/n" could also be read as variance 1/n; the code is unambiguous.) The total variance then converges (to π²/6), so beyond about 4 dimensions their number hardly matters.
- Each cluster applies the 1/i scaling to its own slightly shuffled order of dimensions (each rank perturbed by normal noise with standard deviation 0.1 × dims, Smith's ratio of 10 for 100 dimensions), so camps differ in which issues they vary on most. Cluster centres keep the global order.
- Taken over from Smith: the 1/i scaling and the per-cluster shuffle. Not taken over: his other settings (10 clusters with uniform random weights, cluster centres uniform in ±3/i, a random spread factor per cluster, utility = −distance); our Dirichlet weights, normal centres, one spread and `exp(-distance / width)` stay.
- `--cluster-zipf 0` (the default) gives all dimensions standard deviation 1. Distances are longer there, so without `--cluster-fixed-variance 1` the same width gives steeper preferences.

**Where people stand, and what they care about** (two options of `clusters`):

- `--cluster-fixed-variance 1` scales all positions by one factor so that the dimensions' total variance is π²/6, the limit of Zipf dimensions, whatever their number. Without it and without Zipf, every added dimension makes all distances longer, so preferences get steeper at the same width; with it, more dimensions spread the same disagreement over more issues. (With Zipf the factor is close to 1.)
- Salience per cluster (`--cluster-salience-exponent s`, default 0.5) separates what people care about from where they stand: each cluster weighs the issues 1/i^s (s = 1: 1, 1/2, 1/3, …, Zipf, as Smith's dimensions; s = 0: every issue alike, no salience) in its own random order, in its members' and its candidates' distances, scaled to a mean squared weight of 1. Society can then disagree on many issues while each group mainly cares about a few, and groups differ in which. The positions are the same with and without it (its random draws come last).
- Meant together with `--cluster-zipf 0`: positions spread alike on every issue, salience differs by group. This is the default of `clusters`: `--cluster-count 20 --cluster-zipf 0 --cluster-fixed-variance 1 --cluster-salience-exponent 0.5`. (Until 2026-10-17 a separate `--cluster-salience none|cluster` switched it; exponent 0 gives exactly the electorates of `none`.)

**Scale-free fields** (`--model field`, experimental). Instead of imposing clusters or decay laws, opinions arise as fields over a social space, following what is known about how they spread:

- People live on a two-dimensional social space (who meets whom; a 64 × 64 torus), at places drawn from one Lévy flight of 1000 steps, so they are clustered at all scales.
- Each issue is a Gaussian random field over that space with power spectrum k⁻²: correlations that fall off with the logarithm of distance, as Borghesi & Bouchaud (2010, "Spatial correlations in vote statistics: a diffusive field model for decision-making") found in real vote statistics, i.e. structure at all scales without a characteristic one.
- A person's opinion on an issue is the field where they live (40% of the variance; Borghesi & Bouchaud find a third to a half shared) plus an own part, passed through tanh(`--field-kappa` × ·): a saturating response, as a spin in the Ising model. Small values give smooth, nearly Gaussian opinions; large values push every issue towards two camps.
- `--dims` issues with a fixed total variance (as `--cluster-fixed-variance` for clusters). `--field-salience g` makes what people care about a field too (weights exp(g × another field)), so neighbours share their priorities partly.
- The exponent, the shared share and the Lévy exponent are fixed in `models.py` (`FIELD_EXPONENT`, `FIELD_SHARED`, `FIELD_LEVY`): in first tests they hardly changed the electorates' properties. `--field-kappa` is the knob that matters: from the smooth end (like ks) to the polarised end (like clusters).
- Issues are independent fields, so no ideology (opinions on different issues going together) is built in; how to let it emerge (overlapping topics, Baumann et al. 2021) is open.

**Quinn's hierarchical model** (`--model ks`), from Jameson Quinn's VSE simulations ([vse-sim](https://github.com/electionscience/vse-sim), `src/vse_sim/voter_models.py`, `KSModel`, "kitchen sink"), with the parameters of its published results. In the clusters model a voter belongs to one cluster on all dimensions at once, so clusters behave like consistent ideologies or parties. Here the issues come in bundles, and factions form in each bundle separately, so cleavages cut across each other: a voter can be in the big faction on one bundle and a small one on another, and groups differ in how much they care.

- Issue bundles: the first weighs 1, each further one a random fraction (Beta(1, 3)) of the one before, until the weight falls below `--ks-cut` (0.2); within a bundle, each further dimension weighs a random fraction (Beta(1.5, 3)) of the one before, down to the same cut. So the number of bundles and dimensions varies by election (with the defaults mostly 2–4 dimensions in all), and few issues matter much.
- Factions, in each bundle independently: a Chinese restaurant process over the voters (with `--cands-vote 0`: over the candidates, then the voters). Each person starts a new faction with probability α / (i + α), i the number of persons before, else joins the faction of a random earlier person (α = `--ks-alpha`, 1.5). A few large factions and a tail of small ones; their number is not fixed.
- Each faction, per bundle: a position (normal, with a random variance c ~ Beta(3, 1.5)) and a caring level (Beta(3, 1.5)). Its members sit around its position, the tighter the more it cares (offset N(0, 1) × √(1 − caring) per dimension), and weigh that bundle by their caring.
- Distance: each person weighs each dimension by its importance × their faction's caring on that bundle, normalised per person (as in vse-sim). Utilities use `exp(-distance / width)` like every model here (vse-sim uses −distance), so the models differ only in the electorate's structure. With `--cands-vote 0` candidates are drawn exactly like voters; the two candidate parameters below apply as for any model.
- Two versions. `ks` is the lab's version, which may evolve: a change comes as a new parameter whose default keeps the earlier behaviour (so older runs stay comparable), or under a new model name. `--model ks-quinn` is kept as close to vse-sim's code as the lab allows and is not to be changed: it uses vse-sim's one random offset per person and bundle (see the last point) and has no `--ks-shared`. Both read `--ks-alpha` and `--ks-cut`. What still differs from vse-sim in both is common to every model here: utilities `exp(-distance / width)` instead of −distance, and the candidate parameters.
- `--ks-shared 1` (a test variant of `ks`, not in vse-sim): the factions of the first bundle are used in every bundle, so cleavages no longer cut across each other; positions and caring are still drawn per bundle.
- Written anew from the algorithm; `ks` has one change: vse-sim's code gives a person one random offset for all dimensions of a bundle (it reads the offset by bundle number), which looks unintended; here each dimension has its own. With the exp utilities and this change, results are not directly comparable with Quinn's published numbers. `--dims`, `--cluster-count`, `--cluster-spread` and `--cluster-zipf` do not apply to this model.

**Looking at the electorates.** `show_electorates.py` draws the electorates of one or more models without running any method, in a few seconds, to check by eye that they look as intended and to compare models:

```
python show_electorates.py --model clusters ks ks-quinn --out look       # -> output/look/
python show_electorates.py --model ks --ks-cut 0.05 --width 0.5 --out look-ks
python show_electorates.py --utility exp linear quadratic --out look-utility
```

It takes run.py's electorate parameters (one value each; `--model` and `--utility` may list several, and every model is shown with every utility form) and the same random streams, so election e of a seed is the electorate run.py uses. It writes `examples.png` (a few elections per model: voters coloured by their favourite and the lettered candidates, on the voters' first two principal components; the Condorcet winner ringed), `summary.png` (over 300 elections: variance explained by the first principal components and how many reach 70%, favourite ÷ least liked (with linear or quadratic utility, which are negative, the utilities stretched per voter instead), the utilities, the distances, and the VSE lost by electing the Condorcet winner where it is not the utility winner) and `summary.txt` (the same as numbers). For the ks models, whose people weigh the dimensions differently, positions are scaled by the voters' average weights first: the space roughly as an average voter sees it.

With `--sav` it also maps the candidates the way Alós-Ferrer & Granić (2015, "Political space representations with approval data", *Electoral Studies* 39) mapped real parties and candidates from approval ballots in German field experiments, after Laslier's spatial approval voting (2006). This gives a dimensionality comparable with theirs (four or more dimensions to explain 70% for real voters):

```
python show_electorates.py --model clusters ks --cands 9 --sav --out look-sav
```

- Each electorate is drawn with `--sav-voters` voters (1000), and every voter approves honestly, as in the methods (Honest approval below: a personal threshold between their mean and their favourite; until 2026-10-17 everyone above their mean). `--sav-noise s` (default 1) first blurs each utility by a normal error of s × the voter's own spread of utilities, so approvals are partly chance, as in real ballots (candidates a voter hardly knows, mistakes). Without it the model's approvals are so consistent that, in most elections, some pair of candidates is approved together by nobody, which the map can only patch (below); at 1 no such pair remains and the map comes closer to the real ones (2026-10-17; [journal.md](journal.md), Laziness and approval against real ballots).
- For each pair of candidates, the overlap of the people approving them, relative to chance: r = N · a(c, d) / (a(c) · a(d)). Squared distances are proportional to ln(r_max / r) (the paper's formula with its smallest admissible α; the most overlapping pair is at 0). Classical multidimensional scaling then gives the inertia each dimension explains; only positive eigenvalues count.
- A pair nobody approves together would be infinitely far apart; it is given half a voter instead, and the share of elections where this happens is reported. Without noise this happens in most elections, which makes the map less reliable.
- `sav.png` and `summary.txt` show the cumulative inertia by number of dimensions next to the paper's four data sets (Messel: 9 parties, 8 candidates; Konstanz: 11 parties, 6 candidates). Use a similar number of candidates (`--cands`), since n candidates allow at most n − 1 dimensions.

**Candidates unlike the electorate.** Two parameters make the field unlike the voters. With candidates who vote (the default, below) they choose who stands; with `--cands-vote 0` they move the drawn candidates, after all random draws. Either way the voters are the same for every value, so a sweep compares the same electorates with different fields.

- `--cand-dispersion s` (default 1): the candidates' distance from the voters' mean position is s times the voters'. Below 1 the candidates cluster toward the centre of the electorate; above 1 they are more extreme than the voters. This is the relative candidate dispersion of Merrill (1984), "A comparison of efficiency of multicandidate electoral systems", *American Journal of Political Science* 28, whose Condorcet efficiency of plurality and IRV falls as candidates cluster (centre squeeze: the central candidate is squeezed out of the first choices).
- `--cand-shift d` (default 0): the candidates lie d standard deviations of the voters further along the first, most important dimension: a systematic bias, e.g. candidates more often from one social group.
- Both change how far the best candidate is from the random ballot, the zero of VSE and WME. With a strong shift, one candidate is nearly everyone's favourite, the random ballot is almost as good as the best candidate, and the efficiencies' denominators become small; compare methods within a condition, and use CE or raw rates across conditions.

**Candidates who vote** (`--cands-vote 1`, the default since 2026-11-03): the candidates are voters too, chosen among the `--voters`, as only those allowed to vote may usually stand. 99 voters with 6 candidates are then 99 people, 6 of whom stand. The model draws the voters only, so the electorate is the same for any number of candidates. A candidate's preferences, used by nomination, campaigns and the methods where candidates cast votes, are their own row as a voter (with their own salience, where the model has it). A clone (strategic nomination) is a platform, not a person, and does not vote; a candidate who withdraws still votes. With `--cands-vote 0` the candidates are people apart from the voters, as until then.

*Who stands* (`models.standing`). Without bias the first `--cands` voters stand: a random sample of the electorate, the same for any number of voters, and the 10 candidates of a larger field include the 6. With `--cand-dispersion` or `--cand-shift` the voters are taken in order and each stands with a probability that favours the positions asked for, until the field is full (each voter's draw to stand comes from a stream of their own). The probability is the weight w of an exponential tilt, log w = (d / s²) z₁ − (1/s² − 1) |z|² / 2 for shift d, dispersion s and the voter's position z standardised by the electorate's mean and SD per dimension, divided by its largest value. On a Gaussian electorate this selects candidates distributed exactly as the voters shifted by d and scaled by s, which is what the two parameters do to drawn candidates. Where the weight grows without bound it is cut off: with a shift, voters 3 SDs or more out toward it always stand (`STAND_CAP`); spreading out (s above 1), voters 2 SDs out on average over the dimensions (`STAND_SPREAD`), so a Gaussian electorate gives about 1.33 for s = 1.5. If too few voters stand, the field is filled with the voters of the largest weight. Measured on the defaults (noise model, 6 dimensions, 99 voters, 300 elections), the candidates' mean on the first dimension and their root-mean-square distance from the centre per dimension, in voter SDs:

| Setting | mean, dimension 1 | distance from the centre | field filled |
|---|---|---|---|
| unbiased | −0.02 | 0.99 | — |
| shift 0.5 | +0.50 | 1.01 | 0% |
| shift 1 | +1.04 | 1.13 | 17% |
| dispersion 0.5 | +0.01 | 0.46 | 89% |
| dispersion 2 | −0.05 | 1.81 | 91% |

A field clustered or spread out on all six dimensions at once is rare among 99 people, so it is mostly the most central or the most extreme voters available. With bias the candidates depend on the number of voters only through the electorate's mean and SD (and through filling).

With many voters candidates who vote change little: in the noise model candidates and voters come from the same cloud anyway, so only the few voters who stand differ. It matters where the voters are few, e.g. a committee choosing a chair among its members (`--voters 7 --cands 7`): there every vote is also a stake, sincere Plurality is a seven-way tie (each votes for themselves), and the random ballot, the zero of VSE, becomes a random candidate. Methods with features over every subset of candidates limit the field to about 12.

## Ballots and sincere voting

| Ballot | Form | Sincere ballot |
|---|---|---|
| one name | one candidate | the favourite |
| ranking | ranking; may be truncated (the top k ranked, the rest unranked, tied last) | by utility, complete (truncated with `--lazy`) |
| approval | approve or not | the candidates above the voter's personal approval threshold, between their mean utility and their favourite (see Honest approval below; at least the favourite; with `--lazy` only those the voter cares about) |
| score 0–max | integer score per candidate (`--max-score`, default 5) | stretched to the voter's own utility range, rounded (with `--lazy` the others blank = 0) |
| two-round, plurality first round | one candidate marked for round 1, plus a ranking for the separate runoff | the favourite marked; ranking by utility |
| two-round, approval first round | approvals for round 1, plus a ranking for the separate runoff | approvals as for the approval ballot; ranking by utility |
| veto | one candidate against | the least liked |
| ranking, ties allowed | levels, equal = tied; level 0 shared by two or more = unranked (truncation) | strict ranking by utility (ties only arise from learning; truncated with `--lazy`) |
| truncated ranking | the top k ranked, the rest unranked (tied last) | rank the candidates the voter would approve (as for the approval ballot; with `--lazy`: only those the voter also cares about) |
| ranking of at most 3 | up to 3 ranked, the rest unranked (tied last) | the 3 best-liked (all, with 3 or fewer candidates; with `--lazy`: only those the voter also cares about) |
| full ranking (Inverse-\* methods) | full strict ranking, never truncated | by the reversed preferences |
| three grades | good / OK / bad per candidate | stretched to the voter's own utility range, rounded to 0–2 (as the score ballot, with three levels; not affected by `--max-score`) |

**Honest approval.** Each voter has a personal approval threshold between their mean utility and their favourite: on utilities stretched to 0–1, mean + d × (1 − mean), with d drawn per voter and election uniformly on 0–1 (own random stream, the same for every method; a strategic group's reference ballot uses its members' mean d). So a candidate's chance of being approved rises linearly from 0 at the voter's mean to 1 at the favourite: a gradient, not one cut for everyone, and an approval says how much the voter likes a candidate on average (in expectation the approval ballot is a score ballot above the voter's mean, randomly rounded). It applies to the approval ballot, the round-1 approvals of the approval runoffs and the truncated ranking (MDDA and MAMPO, where ranked = approved). Why: real voters approve 1.5–3 candidates almost regardless of the field (approval elections and experiments with 4–16 candidates), while the earlier rule, every candidate above the voter's mean, approved about 0.4 per candidate (6.4 of 16); strategy removed only a tenth of that. Of the rules compared on the whole curve of sorted approval shares in 11 real elections, this one fitted best, without a fitted constant, a little better than vse-sim's BulletyApproval60 (60% bullet votes) ([journal.md](journal.md), Laziness and approval against real ballots). Until 2026-10-17 the threshold was the voter's mean; it changed the honest results of the approval methods considerably (Approval's honest VSE about 78 → 87).

**Truncation and laziness.** Rankings may be truncated: the voter ranks the top k candidates and leaves the rest unranked, tied last (a ranking of all but one is the full ranking). Strategic voters can truncate (rank only down to the leader they prefer, or bullet-vote for it) and the random search can rank one candidate more or fewer. Truncation reverses no preference, so it does not lower sincerity; it lowers fidelity (less is said). How the methods treat unranked candidates:

| Methods | Unranked candidates |
|---|---|
| pairwise (Schulze, Ranked pairs, Minimax family, Kemeny-Young, Copeland, Copeland//Margins, Baldwin, Nanson, SICT, Condorcet-random-drop, …) | below every ranked candidate, tied with each other (no preference between them) |
| IRV, ER-IRV, Benham, BTR-IRV, Coombs (first choices) | a ballot with no ranked candidate left is exhausted and counts for nobody; majorities are of the continuing ballots |
| Coombs (last places) | the unranked share the last place equally |
| Borda, Black, BTR-Borda | 0 points; ranked candidates keep their full points (C−1, C−2, …), so truncating can pay |
| Dowdall | 0 points |
| Supplementary vote | a ballot with only one candidate ranked has no second preference |
| TRASH (last places) | shared, as in Coombs |

In the ER variants (rankings with ties) a tie at level 0 of two or more candidates means unranked; ties higher up keep the equal-ranking rules (in ER-IRV each candidate tied at the top among the remaining gets a whole vote). The Inverse-\* methods keep full strict rankings: their honest ballot is the reversed one, and truncating it has no clear meaning.

**Laziness** (`--lazy λ`, 0–1) makes honest ballots truncated:

- **Each voter's own cut.** Each voter has a cut c, drawn per election uniformly between 0 and λ (own random stream, the same for every method). They rank a candidate only if its utility, stretched to 0–1 between their least and most liked candidate, is at least c (the favourite always). c = 0 gives a full ranking, c = 1 a bullet vote; in between the length depends on the voter's preferences (a voter with one clear favourite ranks fewer than one who likes several about equally).
- **What λ means.** λ = 0 gives full ballots, λ = 0.5 voters from complete to cutting at the middle of their range, λ = 1 the whole spread down to bullet votes. Voters differ, as on real ballots; a single cut for everyone made every format say roughly "these candidates are acceptable", and the methods alike (until 2026-10-16; [journal.md](journal.md), Laziness per voter).
- **Per ballot type.** It applies to every ballot that can say more than one thing. Rankings (ranking, ranking with ties, truncated ranking, ranking of at most 3; for the last two on top of their own cut) are truncated. Approval ballots (also the round-1 approvals of the approval runoffs) approve only the candidates above their threshold that the voter also cares about, so a cut of 1 is a bullet vote. Score and grade ballots leave the other candidates blank, which counts as 0, so a cut of 1 scores only the favourite. Plurality, veto and the TTR mark already name a single candidate. The Inverse-\* methods are never lazy.
- **Which value.** The default is 0 (full ballots). Real ranked ballots ([journal.md](journal.md), Laziness and approval against real ballots) suggest about 0.1 for an engaged electorate in a Condorcet election with few candidates (Debian) and 0.2–0.5 for public and organisational STV elections with many candidates (Irish STV elections, ERS data), so 0.1 and 0.5 are sensible values for a robustness check. Real ballots also have a few percent of bullet votes even where almost everyone else ranks fully, which one uniform cut cannot produce. IRV ballots are no guide: truncation there is partly strategic (lower preferences rarely count), which the strategic voters already model.
- **Honest ballot and strategy.** The lazy ballot is the voter's honest ballot: what non-strategic voters cast, round 0, and the base of VSE and WME. A strategic group's reference ballot (where its random search starts) uses the mean cut of its members. Strategic voters may rank more or fewer when it pays; kind voters only for a strict gain, so laziness persists unless strategy rewards completing the ranking.

**Two-round elections** (TTR, Approval runoff, Approval cond. runoff, Score runoff) have a real, separate runoff: voters come back and vote between the two finalists, independently of their first vote. With two candidates, voting for the preferred one is always best, so the runoff is decided by the voters' sincere preferences between the finalists. The ballot carries that sincere ranking as a fixed part; strategy changes only the round-1 marks (or scores). Strategic voters can aim their round-1 vote at the runoff with a push-over (ranking groups: Push-over, under Strategy engine; individual voters: compromise for a third candidate with burial of the runner-up). With laziness (`--lazy`), the runoff ranking is truncated as a lazy ranking is: a voter who would leave both finalists unranked does not vote in the runoff (since 2026-10-28; before, every voter voted in every runoff). That is the only turnout change between the rounds the model has. Methods that read the runoff from the same ballot (Supplementary vote, STAR, the BTR methods) are automated runoffs, where strategic voters can also misrank the finalists.

### Score range

`--max-score m` sets the range of the score ballot to 0–m for every method that uses it: the `max-score` set ([methods.md](methods.md), Groups and sets), which is built from the ballot types and so includes add-ons on score ballots. The default 0–5 is an implementation choice, not part of these methods; other simulations (e.g. Quinn's VSE) also run 0–10 and 0–100. With a coarse range, sincere voters must give candidates they like differently the same score, so pairwise preferences are lost: this is the cap on WME seen for the rated and hybrid methods. A wider range reduces it. MCA and 321 keep their three grades, and methods on other ballots are unaffected; the electorates are the same for every value, so a sweep (`sweep.py --max-score 5 10 100`) isolates the effect of the range, and its chain plot draws the reachable frontier.

Rules that refer to the range scale with it: ARA approves ratings above the middle, ARA-Top only the top score, Score+ stretches to the full range, Reciprocal score measures support as a share of the top score. Learning: see the score mutation in Strategy engine.

**Global range and fixed range.** `--max-score` is the global range: every method on score ballots follows it, unless the method fixes its own range with `settings`, e.g. `Method("score", d_sum, ("sum",), settings={"max_score": 10})`. While such a method's ballots are made, changed (strategy) and tallied, its own range is in force; afterwards the global one again. A method with a fixed range is named after it, `Name-X` (`Score-10`), and its rows still show the run's global `max_score`, so the name is what tells them apart. Avoid fixing the range for the base version of a method: a base method that follows `--max-score` stays comparable across runs, and a max-score sweep varies it like all the others (a fixed one stays the same at every value of the sweep). A test checks that every base method follows the global range and that fixed ones are named `Name-X`. A fixed method is equivalent to its base version at that global range: `Score-10` in a run at `--max-score 5` gives exactly the rows of `Score` at `--max-score 10`. No built-in method fixes its range at present. The settings a method can fix are listed in `SETTINGS` in methods.py (so far only `max_score`).

## Strategy engine

Strategic voters answer polls before the vote (`strategy.py`). Two engines do this, on the same polls:

- **Individual voters** (`--individual 1`, the default since 2026-10-31; individual.py): every voter decides alone, from moves found for voters who see the race as they do, counted on the published polls (pooled discovery, the default since 2026-11-06; until then by arithmetic on the poll totals).
- **Ranking groups** (`--individual 0`, the engine of version 1.0.0): voters with the same sincere ranking answer as one group, by their expected gain over the polls, recounted with their ballot changed.

The front-runners, the voters answering around them and the attention to both sides (attack and defence) take inspiration from John Huang's votesim ([summary report](http://votesim.usa4r.org/summary-report.html), [code](https://github.com/johnh865/election_sim)), which measures one-sided strategy (only the challenger's coalition uses tactics) and two-sided strategy (the honest winner's supporters answer with a bullet vote). Version 1.0.0 also had **convergent learning** (`--converge N`), a long-run model where groups take turns over many rounds; it was removed on 2026-10-29 and remains available in that version.

### Poll rounds (both engines)

One election, with a few polls before it. Voters see only published poll results, never anyone's preferences, and answer them all at the same time.

- **Round 0:** everyone votes sincerely (naive, uninformed). `--rounds 0` reports this as the strategic result.
- **Round r** (1 … `--rounds`, default 3): a poll of round r−1 is published, and every strategic voter (or group) answers it at once. Nobody sees anyone else's answer before the next poll. The strategic axes report the last round. Each round answers a pure poll of the round before (level-k reasoning: round 1 best-responds to sincere voters, round 2 to round-1 voters, …).
- **What a poll shows:** the method's own counts, the summed ballot features it decides from (first preferences for Plurality, approvals for Approval, the pairwise matrix for Condorcet methods, score totals and head-to-heads for STAR, …), from a random sample of `--poll-size` voters (default half the electorate, rounded to an odd number: 99 → 49, 100 → 51, 200 → 101), scaled up to the electorate. `--samples` independent polls (5; 10 until 2026-10-26) stand for the uncertainty. A poll shows only totals a real poll could show: per candidate, per pair of candidates, or per candidate and grade (since 2026-10-29). Methods counted from totals over every subset of candidates are counted by the voters from such totals with a stand-in (`POLL` in methods.py): the IRV family (IRV, ER-IRV, IRV-3, BTR-IRV, Benham, Coombs, Inverse-IRV, Inverse-ER-IRV) from first preferences and a transfer table (on the ballots with X first, who comes next), passing an eliminated candidate's votes on by it; DSC as Plurality, DAC as Approval; Reciprocal score with factions by single favourite; Score+, INS, ESR and Geometric median as Score; MARS-T as MARS; Soft Minimax-L and -D as Soft Minimax; Condorcet-random-drop as Minimax (the Condorcet winner, else Minimax). Add-ons counted from such totals without a stand-in of their own are polled as Score (score ballots) or by first preferences. With three candidates the IRV stand-ins give exactly the method's result; with six, in spatial electorates, the same winner in about 85% of polls for IRV and Coombs (BTR-IRV 99%, Benham 100%). The election itself is always counted by the method. Until then the polls showed the method's own counts, which for the IRV family meant every elimination order, more than a real poll shows.
- **Uncertainty:** voters treat a poll as `--poll-error` times (2) less precise than its sampling error: the polls are spread around the true count by that factor. Real polls miss results by about twice their stated sampling error (Shirani-Mehr, Rothschild, Goel & Gelman 2018). With the sampling error alone, the leader's supporters almost never see a poll in which they are behind, so (for the ranking groups) they never protect their lead, while the runner-up's supporters attack it.
- **Settled:** the last round gave the same ballots as the round before (a fixed point).
- Condorcet-random-drop, which has no feature sums, is polled by tallying the sampled ballots (for the ranking groups with the group's sampled members changed).

### Individual voters (the default)

`--individual 1` with pooled discovery (`--response pooled`), the default since 2026-11-06 (individual.py, `answer_pooled`). The rule in short:

> Discovery is shared, the decision individual, as with voters who learn from pundits and campaigns what pays under a method but decide for themselves. The voters are grouped by how they see the race: their order of the poll's top three candidates. For each such type, the obvious moves are tried on the published polls: what happens if a share `--coordination` of the type makes the move, counted by the method itself. Each voter then weighs what their type's moves promise with their own utilities.
>
> Voting insincerely has a personal cost, different for each voter: some never would, some always do, most only when it is worth it. A voter makes the best move if its value exceeds that cost, and votes sincerely otherwise. A move that disappointed in the last poll is trusted less.

So there is one comparison per voter, value against cost, and what makes a move attractive enters only through its value: how much the voter cares (their utilities), how the method's count responds to the move on the polls (with their spread), and how many act alike (a share of their type). In detail:

- **Polls and places.** The round's `--samples` polls, each spread by `--poll-error` as above, as the ranking groups see them. The top three (the two leaders and the next place) are the most frequent winners of the polls, found as for the ranking groups (Leaders).
- **Types.** The voters by their order of the top three: six types. The top three are those likely to win, and the six orders cover the voters' preferences among them.
- **Moves.** For each type, the moves of the ranking groups: the naive exaggerations for the leader the type prefers against the other (`moves._naive`: one name: it; approval: up to it, or everything below it out; scores: the top up to it and the lowest from the other down, or the top up to it and the lowest below it; rankings: it first and the other last, or one of the two) and the push-over for the next place (`moves._push`). Every voter of the type turns a move into their own ballot, from their own utilities.
- **Valuing a move.** A share `--coordination` (0.25) of the type's change of ballots (from what they voted in the last round) is added to each poll, and the polls are counted again by the method: the winner chances the move promises. The type returning to its sincere ballots is counted the same way, and a move's value for a voter is the gain in their expected utility over that, as a share of their own utility range. Nobody sees individual ballots: only published totals are counted, which anyone knowing the method can do. Every voter of a type counts in its change, strategic or not, since discovery cannot know who follows. Because the method itself counts, ties, cycles, eliminations and stage effects all show; no method needs a rule of its own.
- **How many polls.** With few polls each move's value is a coarse estimate, and a voter taking the best of several such values favours lucky ones: 5 polls give about half again as much strategic voting as 40, and the results settle from 20. The default stays at 5 on purpose: overreacting to noisy information is part of what a method has to withstand, and some methods are more sensitive to it than others (IRV most: its WME\* was 6 points lower with 5 polls than with 20). The reference runs test 10 and 20 (journal, 2026-11-06). A second look (choosing a move with the round's polls and valuing it with a fresh set) overcorrected and is left off (`individual.POOL_CHECK`).
- **Search** (`--search` k, default 0): each type also tries k random proposals per round, each one random step after its best move so far, as the ranking groups search; a proposal that raises the type's mean gain is kept in the type's library (the 8 most recent, moves of up to 6 steps) and offered again in later rounds. It changes the results clearly only for the IRV family, whose hard-to-predict count rewards odd moves, and there for the worse; the named moves cover the rest (journal, 2026-11-06).
- **Cost.** Each voter's cost of voting insincerely, as a share of their utility range: always strategic voters 0 (`--strategic`), sincere voters never move, adaptive voters (the default, everyone else) their personal draw, uniform from 0 to `--honesty` (0.3), from their own random stream per election (the same for every method). The voter makes the most valuable move if its value exceeds their cost; ties go to the sincere ballot. `--honesty` sets how much strategy there is. It was set with the arithmetic (below): under Plurality, at the defaults, 0.3 let about 12% of the voters whose favourite is not one of the two poll leaders desert them, and about 5% of all voters move per poll, close to what surveys find in plurality elections (4–8% of all voters in Canada; of the supporters of the party third in their district, one in eight, Blais 2002); 1 gave about a third of that, 0.1 about twice. With pooled discovery about 4% of all voters move per poll under Plurality (300 elections), still in that range.
- **Judging.** Each voter remembers what their move promised: the gain in expected outcome over the polls as they were. The next poll shows what happened. The ratio, from −3 (backfired three times as badly as promised) to 1 (kept its promise), updates their trust in what the move aims at (each candidate it raises or lowers), as a running average with a prior of one kept promise. A promise counts only as far as it is trusted; trust never makes a harmful move look good. The poll cannot tell a voter's own move from everyone else's, so the judgement is as crude as real voters' is.
- **Campaigns** (`--campaign 1`) need the arithmetic (`--response arithmetic`): pooled discovery has none yet.
- **Not available** with individual voters: `--aggressive` (no effect) and `--risk` (refused). The cost takes their place.
- **Settled**, **random streams**: as for the ranking groups. Discovery and the candidates' search draw from a stream of their own, so the polls stay those of a run without them.

**The arithmetic** (`--response arithmetic`, the default from 2026-10-31 to 2026-11-06). Every voter decides alone from the round's poll totals, by arithmetic on them, and considers compromise and burial around the top three:

- **The poll of polls.** The round's `--samples` polls (each spread by `--poll-error` as above) are averaged and read as one poll of their combined size.
- **Closeness, one rule for every method.** For each candidate c: the smallest number of voters switching from the poll winner A to c that makes c win the count of the totals. A switching voter turns a ballot supporting A over c into the same ballot with A and c swapped (rankings: A first, c second; one name: A to c; approval: A alone to c alone; scores: the top score from A to c). The runner-up B is the closest candidate, the next place T the next closest. No method needs a margin of its own.
- **Chances.** P(c) = Φ(−k_c / σ) for B and T, with k_c the closeness and σ = `--poll-error` × the sampling error of a two-way split, in voters (n × 0.5 / √(polls × poll size)); A has the rest. A move can take k_c below 0 (c then leads, with a chance above 1/2), the same in attack and in defence.
- **Moves.** Compared with the sincere ballot:
  - compromise for one of A, B and T, supporting it as far as the format allows without burying anyone (one name: that candidate; approval: down to it; scores: the top down to it; rankings: it first);
  - burial of one of them: it and everyone liked no more to the bottom, where the format allows (the lowest score, not approved, unranked or tied last); strict rankings can only put it last, below candidates liked less; none for one name, two-round marks and veto;
  - both, compromise for one and burial of another (for a voter preferring A, compromise for T with burial of B is the push-over).
- **Valuing a move.** The change of the voter's own ballot, from what they voted in the last round, is worth so many voters switching from A to c: its projection on the change one switching voter makes to the totals. Under Plurality, a vote moving from T to B is half a switch, from A to B a whole one; under a Condorcet method, putting B first when B was already above A changes nothing in the A–B race. The voter expects f voters to make it: themselves and `--coordination` (0.25) of the others whose favourite is theirs (`individual.bloc`). The poll does not show this, but the size of one's own camp is what a voter can judge; a camp then acts alike, as Duverger's deserting voters do. So c needs that many fewer voters (in defence: more), and the chances follow. The value is the gain in expected utility over the sincere ballot, as a share of the voter's own utility range.
- **Cross-effects.** The races move each other. With the poll, a scenario table is read: B's closeness again for x voters switching from A to T (x from −24 to +24), and the same for T with voters switching to B. A move's worth in one race is carried into the other through it. Under a runoff method, T passing B in the first round can put B out of the runoff, so a push-over pays where the poll says so. Under a Condorcet method, T beating A can make a cycle that B wins, so burial of A under T can pay through it.
- **Discovery** (`--search`, default 0): each voter who may move also tries that many random variations of the move they hold, valued and judged like every move. In its first trial it changed nothing beyond noise and cost about a third more run time, so it is off by default.
- **Campaigns** (`--campaign 1`): candidates recommend moves to their audience, planned with `--coordination` of it following; each voter weighs the recommendation of the candidate they like most among those campaigning, if their own part alone does not harm them ([campaigns.md](campaigns.md), Campaigns for individual voters).
- **Why it was replaced.** The arithmetic is close to exact for additive counts (Plurality, Approval, Score, Borda), but not for counts where the winner depends on several quantities at once: pairwise counts, eliminations, multi-stage counts. There a ballot change acts on combinations the switches from A do not cover. The response check (`response_check.py`, [response_check.md](../response_check.md)) measured it: under Condorcet methods the arithmetic sees a gain in well under 1% of the moves where counting again finds one in about 2.5%, and under multi-stage rated methods it promises gains that are not there in 12–21%. So it favoured some counts over others, Copeland and Baldwin most: there voters almost never saw a third candidate in the race.
- **Exact response, a variant for analysis** (`--response exact`, since 2026-11-06): a move is valued by counting the averaged poll again with the change and finding the closeness of B and T by the same bisection as for the poll itself (for a candidate who leads after the change: how far, down to 3 sigma below 0). Everything else stays: the same moves, races, spread, chances, bloc, cost and trust. It is about six times slower than the arithmetic.
- **Until 2026-11-04** an adaptive voter first needed a stake (|u(A) − u(B)| × 4 P(A) P(B)) above their draw, a boldness drawn per voter from 1 to `--coordination` of the electorate, and a move worth more than the poll's uncertainty could account for (its gain larger than the change if the poll were off by σ), with chances never projected past an even race. Those filters were chained and partly counted the same thing twice (the closeness of the race in all three, the poll's uncertainty in the chances and the test), which left few voters strategic. They also carved out fixed sets of voters: a voter expecting fewer than about 0.75 σ followers never acted, whatever the stake, so `--coordination 0` gave no strategy at all and the share left out depended on the number of voters, and in near ties the runner-up's supporters rarely acted while the leader's still defended ([journal.md](journal.md)).

### Ranking groups

`--individual 0`, the engine of version 1.0.0 (strategy.py).

- **Groups.** Whole ranking groups (strategic voters with the same sincere ranking, no cap). Voters who share a ranking and see the same poll reach the same answer, so they act as a bloc. Factions across rankings are not built in; they emerge when groups answer the same poll alike (all who prefer the runner-up to the leader, say).
- **Leaders.** The two candidates that win most often over the round's polls, the same for everyone. If one candidate wins every poll, the polls are widened (deviations × 2, 4, …; counts that would cross zero are clipped there) until another one wins somewhere: the race that would matter first.
- **Answer.** A group estimates the count for a ballot b as poll count − its own previous ballots + its size × b, and judges b by its expected utility gain over its sincere ballots, over the polls. It first tries a few candidate ballots: its previous answer, and the naive exaggerations of its ballot type around the two leaders (A = the one it prefers, B = the other):

| Ballot | Naive exaggerations |
|---|---|
| one name | vote A |
| approval | approve everyone liked at least as much as A |
| score, three grades | top grade for everyone liked at least as much as A, bottom for everyone liked no more than B, stretched in between; or top down to A and bottom below |
| ranking | A to the top and B to the bottom; A to the top; B to the bottom |
| ranking with ties | everyone liked at least as much as A tied at the top, with or without B at the bottom; or the ranking moves |
| truncated ranking | rank only down to A, sincerely or with A first |
| ranking of at most 3 | A first and B never ranked; or rank only down to A (at most 3) |
| two-round, plurality first round | mark A (the runoff ranking stays sincere) |
| two-round, approval first round | approve everyone liked at least as much as A (the runoff ranking stays sincere) |
| veto | veto B |

**Aggressive** voters take the best of these if it does not lose in the polls: exaggeration wins ties, they exaggerate when it cannot hurt, whatever the gap. **Kind** voters take it only if it strictly gains: honesty wins ties. `--aggressive` (0.5) is the share of strategic voters who are aggressive; each voter's type is drawn per election from its own random stream (the same for every method), and aggressive and kind voters form separate groups. They are only candidates: one that backfires in the polls is not taken. Then random search (`--search` proposals, default 1, each a small change of the best ballot so far) takes a change only if it strictly gains. Since the previous answer is the first candidate, the search goes on over the rounds from where it stood: one step per round. (Until 2026-10-29: at most 10 proposals per round, stopping after 3 in a row without gain; a sweep over `--search` 0 to 5 changed the results little.) A group none of whose candidates is safe and whose search finds no gain stays sincere. Without a runner-up (exact polls) there are no exaggerations, only the search.

**Push-over** (since 2026-10-28). Besides the naive exaggerations for the leader they prefer (A, against the other leader B), strategic groups try one push-over: the ballot for A against B with a third candidate X supported as well, so that X rather than B faces A in a runoff or an elimination. X is the next place in the polls, found the way the runner-up is: the candidate other than the two leaders that wins most often, widening the polls until one of the rest wins somewhere (since 2026-10-29; before, every third candidate was tried). Only that one is tried because it is the one most likely to get past B; a push-over for a weaker candidate, who could be pushed past B only with many votes, is left to the random search. Per format: a single mark goes to X; approval approves X too; scores and grades give X the top as well; rankings put X first, A second and B last; truncated rankings rank X first, then down to A; two-round ballots support X in round 1 (TTR: mark X; approval runoffs: approve X too; Score runoff: the top score for X too), the runoff ranking staying sincere. Veto ballots have none. Like every option it is taken only when the polls show it pays, so it matters where a weak opponent can be steered into a later stage: TTR, Supplementary vote, the IRV family. Before, two-round ballots got it on 2026-10-28 and the other formats only through the random search. It costs about 20% more run time (the standard set at the defaults; 45% when every third candidate was tried).

### Partial strategy (both engines)

`--strategic p` makes a random share p of the voters always strategic; the others are adaptive (by default, see Adaptive voters below) or, with `--adaptive 0`, always sincere. Each voter is strategic with probability p, drawn from its own random stream per election, so the same voters are strategic for every method. Ranking groups form only among the strategic voters. p = 1 gives full strategy; p = 0 with no adaptive voters (`--adaptive 0`) gives the sincere results. A share like 0.5 with `--adaptive 0` (the default until 2026-10-17) is an intermediate level, in the spirit of other simulations that report 0, 50 and 100% strategy, and comparable with them. Sincerity and the strategy types are averaged over all voters, including the sincere ones.

With the ranking groups, run time falls less than in proportion to p: halving the strategic voters removes fewer than half of the groups, since many groups are small.

**Adaptive voters** (`--adaptive a`). A fixed share of strategic voters makes some of them optimise their ballot even when nothing is at stake for them, while sincere voters, lazy ones in particular, never react. Adaptive voters strategise when the race that matters matters to them.

- Three groups, from the same random draw per voter: always strategic (a share p, `--strategic`), adaptive (the next share a) and always sincere (the rest; p + a ≤ 1). With `--adaptive 0` the results are exactly those of the share model.
- **The default** (since 2026-10-17): `--strategic 0` and `--adaptive` left out, which means everyone not always strategic is adaptive (a = 1 − p). So by default every voter is adaptive, and `--strategic 0.5` alone gives half always strategic, half adaptive; the share model needs `--adaptive 0`. Why: adaptive voters strategise when they have a reason, need no share to be chosen, and kept the method order of the share model while removing its artefacts (strategy without a stake, sincere voters who never react; [journal.md](journal.md), Adaptive voters).
- **Stake** (ranking groups). In each round, after the poll, the two leaders A and B are known, the same for everyone (Leaders). A voter's stake is |u(A) − u(B)|, rescaled to 0–1 between their least and most liked candidate: how much the outcome of the leading race matters to them. Individual voters instead compare the value of their best move with their draw (times `--honesty`): Individual voters, Cost.
- **Decision, every round anew.** Each adaptive voter has a personal threshold, fixed for the election: their place in the adaptive band of the draw, uniform on 0–1. In a round, the voter votes strategically if their stake exceeds it, and sincerely otherwise (lazy if `--lazy` is set). So with the ranking groups the share strategic at a given stake equals that stake: a voter indifferent between the leaders never strategises, one with everything at stake always does. Individual voters compare the value of their best move with the threshold times `--honesty` instead (Individual voters, Cost). A voter can turn sincere again when the leaders change.
- **Both sides.** In a close race the supporters of both leaders have a stake, so attack and defence come from the same rule; the leader rule of Myerson and Weber needs both. A method whose two leaders are both broadly acceptable gives fewer voters a reason to strategise.
- The report gives the share of adaptive voters voting strategically in the reported round, per method (column `adapted` in the elections CSV).
- `sweep.py` skips combinations with p + a above 1. With `--start previous`, the previous election uses the same groups.

### Coordination: what the strategic axes measure

How many voters move together decides which strategies pay, and the methods differ sharply in this.

- **Compromise** (voting for a viable candidate instead of a hopeless favourite) pays even for a single voter. It is the main strategy in TTR (top-two runoff) and IRV, and it tends to carry the Condorcet winner into the final round.
- **Burial** (ranking or rating a rival below a candidate one likes less, to create a cycle or to drop the rival in a runoff) only pays when many voters do it together. A single voter out of 99 is almost never pivotal for it. It is the main strategy against Condorcet methods, STAR and BTR-score, and it targets exactly the Condorcet winner.

Individual voters coordinate only through what they expect: with pooled discovery, a move is valued as if a share `--coordination` of the voters with the same order of the top three made it (with the arithmetic: of the voters with the same favourite), and with `--campaign 1` candidates offer a common move. With the ranking groups, every voter with the same sincere ranking (and the same attitude, aggressive or kind) acts as one group, so burial pays for a group in proportion to its size; coordination is limited by information instead: one poll per round, and every group answers it at once without seeing the others. With many candidates there are many possible rankings (720 for 6 candidates), so most ranking groups are small, and organised blocs across rankings arise only when groups answer the same poll alike. Coordination by favourite or by cluster would be a different model.

A second, smaller asymmetry: learning is myopic. A burying group does not anticipate that the Condorcet winner's supporters may bury back, or that the cycle it creates can elect a candidate it likes even less (the chicken dilemma). This probably makes burial look somewhat more attractive than it would be to far-sighted voters.

With the ranking groups, run time grows with the number of groups.

### Details

- **Proposals** (one small change to a ballot; `strategy.mutate`; the ranking groups' random search, and the discovery of individual voters):
  - one name: switch to another candidate
  - ranking: swap two candidates
  - approval: toggle one candidate (never empty or full)
  - two-round ballots: move the round-1 mark to another candidate, or toggle one approval (never empty or full); the runoff ranking never changes
  - veto: move the veto to another candidate
  - ranking with ties: move one candidate to any other level (equal levels are ties)
  - truncated ranking: swap two candidates, or rank one more or one fewer
  - score: set one candidate to a different value 0–max. For ranges above 0–5, half of the proposals use the six levels of the 0–5 scale stretched to the range (0, 20, … 100 for 0–100), so the chance of trying an extreme does not shrink as the range grows and learning is not slowed by the range alone
  - three grades: set one candidate to a different grade
- **Feature sums:** every tally except Condorcet-random-drop depends on the ballots only through sums over voters of per-voter features (totals, first and last places, the pairwise matrix, grade counts, first choices among each subset of candidates, ...). Each method lists its features and decides from their sums. A group's change is evaluated by updating the polled sums instead of recounting. All features are integers; poll counts are scaled from a sample and need not be integers.
- **Random streams:** every method learns with a fresh copy of the same random stream per election (common random numbers: the same polls for every method as long as they draw alike), and the candidates' order of moves in strategic nomination is likewise the same for every method. So results do not change when methods are added or reordered, methods that agree on every ballot set agree in the results (IRV and ER-IRV on honest ballots), and paired differences are not blurred by different poll draws.

A candidate who was not in the previous election (a new clone, or an original who had withdrawn) gets nothing there: the lowest value a ballot can give. Candidates not on this field are left out. A ballot keeps its order and levels this way, but not what the format adds beyond them (the runoff ranking of a two-round ballot follows its first-round marks). ## Strategic nomination

`--exits 1` and `--clones N` let the candidates of the initial draw change the field before the vote (`nomination.py`; after Green-Armytage 2014, "Strategic voting and nomination", *Social Choice and Welfare* 42, with clones added). Nobody new enters. Since 2026-11-03 this is the default (`--exits 1 --clones 2`): candidates who would lose by standing, or gain by a copy of themselves or an ally, do act on it, so a fixed field is the less realistic assumption. `--exits 0 --clones 0` gives the field as drawn.

- **Moves.** Exit (at least two original candidates stay in), re-entry after an exit, fielding a clone of any original in the race (themselves included; at most one clone per original and N in the race), withdrawing a clone they fielded. A clone is a copy at a small random offset (0.01 per dimension), so voters rank the clone next to their original and split between the two.
- **Who moves and why.** Candidates sit in the issue space and value a winner as a voter at their position would (by the run's utility; themselves best). With candidates who vote (the default) these are exactly their own preferences as voters. A clone counts exactly as their original, and does not vote. A candidate makes a move only if it strictly improves their expected utility under the method's result with **sincere** ballots on the new field: candidates foresee honest voting, not learned strategy.
- **Dynamics.** Rounds in which the candidates take turns in random order, each making their best improving move. A round without a move ends it (an equilibrium); otherwise it stops after 20 rounds. Each method gets its own field. The voters then vote on that field, honestly and with learned ballots as usual.
- **Baseline.** Results are judged on the **original field**: a clone's chance of winning counts as its original's, and withdrawn candidates stay in the comparison. VSE, WME, the random ballot, the best candidate and the reachable frontier are therefore the same as without nomination, and the change in VSE and WME is what nomination does to the outcome. Withdrawing the best candidate counts as a loss.
- **Report.** With nomination on, each condition gets a table per method: how often the honest result changed (Green-Armytage's measure of vulnerability, here after the dynamics rather than for a single move), the average number of exits and clones at the end, and how often the field settled. A sweep `--exits 0 1` or `--clones 0 2` draws chain plots with the frontier.

- **Why rated methods are hit.** Sincere ballots are stretched between each voter's least and most liked candidate on the field they see. When a candidate withdraws, their supporters give the top score to their next choice, usually the withdrawing candidate's ally, so an exit works like an endorsement. At the defaults, nomination lowers the honest VSE of Score from 90.9 to 87.5 (MAV, STAR and others similarly), all of it from exits; Condorcet methods are unaffected, since removing other candidates cannot unseat a Condorcet winner. With the favourite's end of each voter's scale fixed to the original field, Score stays at 90.8; fixing the least liked end changes nothing, since far-off candidates already share the lowest scores. Whether real voters re-stretch their top score after a withdrawal is open; the model assumes they do ([journal.md](journal.md), 2026-11-06).

Cost: each candidate move is checked with one sincere tally on the changed field, a few hundred tallies per method and election; learning remains the larger part.

## Participation

`--participation` checks the no-show paradox (`participation.py`): could some voters get a better result by staying home? It is a diagnostic; nobody actually abstains, and the strategic results do not change (staying home is not among the strategic voters' options).

- **Who stays home.** Voters with the same sincere ranking and the same ballot form a group. For each group, 1, half (rounded up) or all of its members leave out their ballots, while everyone else votes as before. A single voter is rarely pivotal among 99, so the larger shares matter.
- **Failure.** The resulting winner lottery is better for those who stayed home (expected utility, summed over them) than the one with their ballots. Leaving out a ballot means the whole ballot, in a two-round election both rounds.
- **Both ballot sets.** Honest: everyone votes sincerely (the classical participation criterion, for groups). Strategic: everyone keeps the learned ballots and the group drops its learned ballot; a strategic ballot that backfires shows up here.
- **Report** (when the CSV has the columns): per method, the share of elections with a failure (with a 95% interval), the share of voters in failing groups, and where it fails, the mean of the largest gain per absent voter as a share of that voter's utility range (favourite minus least liked).
- Score, Approval and Borda satisfy participation with sincere ballots, so they fail only with learned ones; IRV, TTR and the Condorcet methods (with 4 or more candidates) can fail with sincere ballots too.

Cost: one tally per group and share (feature sums minus the absent ballots), a few hundred per method and election; with all methods at the defaults the run takes about 5% longer.

## Manipulation test

`--manipulation` asks how bad deliberate, informed manipulation from honest ballots can get (`manipulation.py`), after John Huang's votesim ([summary report](http://votesim.usa4r.org/summary-report.html)) and the vulnerability measure of Green-Armytage, Tideman & Cosman (2016, "Statistical evaluation of voting rules", *Social Choice and Welfare* 46). It is a diagnostic beside the poll rounds: the poll rounds show what typical strategy does, this test the worst a coalition with perfect information can do. It changes no other result.

- **Coalitions.** T is the honest winner (the most likely one if the honest result is a lottery). For every other candidate X, the coalition is every voter who prefers X to T.
- **Tactics.** The coalition tries each naive exaggeration of its ballot type for X against T, the same ones the ranking groups start from (X to the top and T to the bottom of a ranking, approve everyone liked at least as much as X, top score down to X and 0 from T down, …; see Strategy engine, Ranking groups). Every member uses the same tactic on their own utilities; a member whose ballot type offers none keeps their sincere ballot. Everyone else votes sincerely.
- **One-sided.** A tactic succeeds if the coalition gains (expected utility summed over its members). Recorded: whether some coalition succeeds, and the total utility of the successful manipulation that is worst for the electorate; without a success, the honest result.
- **Two-sided.** For each successful tactic, T's supporters (everyone not in the coalition) answer with an exaggeration for T against X: of their ballot type's naive exaggerations, the one that leaves the coalition the least gain, so a manipulation stands only if it pays against every answer. Recorded the same way, among the manipulations that stand. votesim lets the defence bullet-vote and keeps backfired two-sided outcomes too; here the defence tries every exaggeration of its ballot type and only manipulations that still pay count.
- **Report.** Per method: the share of elections in which some coalition can manipulate, one-sided and two-sided (with 95% intervals), and the VSE of the recorded outcomes (ratio of sums, as VSE). Columns `manip1`, `manip2`, `U_manip1`, `U_manip2` in the sincere rows of the elections CSV.
- With strategic nomination, the test runs on the field the voters face and is judged on the original field, like everything else.

Cost: a few tallies per candidate and tactic, a few dozen per method and election; with the standard set a run takes only slightly longer.
